What the generic ballot measures

The question asks: "If the election were held today, would you vote for the Democratic or Republican candidate for Congress in your district?" It is a national aggregate that does not account for district-level variation, incumbency, or candidate quality.

Why Democrats need a larger lead

Democratic voters are geographically concentrated in urban areas, creating "wasted votes" in safe districts. The efficiency gap means Democrats need roughly a +3 to +5 national margin to win a bare majority. In 2012 Democrats won the generic ballot by 1.2 points but lost the House by 33 seats.

Historical accuracy

The final generic ballot average has correctly predicted which party wins the House majority in 8 of the last 10 midterm cycles. It begins to stabilise approximately 6 months before the election and is most predictive in the final 60 days.

Current trajectory

The Democratic lead has widened from roughly even in January 2025 to a consistent +4 to +5 advantage by mid-2026. This trajectory, if sustained, places Democrats in territory that has historically produced net House gains of 15 to 30 seats.

What party favourability measures

Polls ask respondents whether they have a favourable or unfavourable opinion of each political party as an institution. Unlike the generic ballot, this does not ask about voting intent -- it measures underlying brand sentiment that affects long-run electoral positioning.

Why both parties are unpopular

Partisan polarisation has depressed favourability for both parties since the mid-2000s. Voters who strongly identify with one party rate the other very negatively, pulling down aggregate ratings. Independents -- the swing group -- rate both parties below 40%.

Relationship to midterm outcomes

The party with higher relative favourability -- even if both are below 50% -- tends to perform better. More important is the trend: a party whose favourability is falling faster than its opponent's typically underperforms. The Republican decline since January 2025 is a warning signal.

The enthusiasm gap

Low favourability for the governing party tends to energise the opposition base. Negative partisanship -- voting against rather than for -- is a primary driver of midterm turnout. The out-party (Democrats in 2026) historically benefits from this dynamic when in-party favourability is falling.

Why congressional approval matters

Low congressional approval energises challengers and suppresses incumbent turnout. Voters in marginal districts who disapprove of Congress are more likely to vote against the majority party's candidates, even if they personally like their own member.

The paradox of personal vs institutional approval

Voters consistently rate their own representative much higher than Congress as a whole. This "my congressman is fine, but Congress is broken" paradox partially insulates incumbents but creates vulnerability for the majority brand in wave conditions.

119th Congress: a record for dysfunction

Two record-breaking government shutdowns, the fewest bills enacted since records began, a Speaker who survived three removal attempts, and a debt ceiling standoff that lasted 6 weeks -- the 119th Congress has generated more negative headlines than any session since the post-Watergate 94th Congress.

Relationship to the generic ballot

Congressional approval and the generic ballot are correlated but measure different things. Approval measures institutional sentiment; the generic ballot measures voting intent. In 2026 both indicators are pointing in the same direction: away from the Republican majority.
